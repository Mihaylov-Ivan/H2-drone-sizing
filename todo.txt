Structure:
- Wing weight way too small, walls must be thicker. Neglect of Other Loads/Stiffness: The model checks ultimate bending and torsion, but doesn’t explicitly consider wing deflection (flexibility) or buckling of the thin skin. A 0.5 mm ASA skin over a 1 m span wing could flex significantly or buckle under compressive load long before material yield. For a robust prototype, adding margin (thicker skin or additional spars) to limit wing deflection and prevent flutter or buckling is advisable, even if it’s not “required” by static strength equations.
- Need to add wing fairing to weights too
- One thing to keep in mind is that the elevator hinge and tips will need reinforcement in practice, especially with a 1.25 mm skin forward and perhaps only 0.5 mm elevator skin behind – hinge moments could cause stress concentrations.
- The model’s approach (uniform shell * 1.2 factor) will underpredict stress in localized areas but overpredict mass a bit overall. Since this is conceptual, the approach is acceptable, but plan to examine the fuselage after fabrication to ensure it can handle point loads. For instance, 0.5 mm ASA may be too thin to thread screws into or to absorb landing shock if this is a tailsitter landing on its tail. You may opt to overbuild the fuselage slightly (e.g., print with thicker walls at the wing saddle, or increase the safety factor for bending) to avoid cracks in the prototype.
- Compressive buckling of a thin skin can occur at loads well below yield. The analysis doesn’t check this. For example, the top of the wing skin in a high-g turn will be in compression. A 0.5 mm ASA sheet over a 25 mm chord segment might buckle locally. Increasing skin thickness or adding a second spar (creating a shorter panel span) could raise the buckling resistance significantly.
- You don’t want the wing to behave like a floppy plate. Testing a printed section of the wing for flex might be worthwhile – if it flexes too easily by hand, consider design changes before full build.
- Vibration could loosen or fatigue the mounts. Using 1.25 mm skin and carbon spars in the tail should help. Ensure the motor mount design (e.g., how the motor attaches to the tail) spreads the load. A small mounting plate or doubler at that location could prevent cracks in the ASA due to vibrations. The analysis doesn’t explicitly cover this, so it falls under prototyping practices.
